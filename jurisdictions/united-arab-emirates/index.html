Logistics and supply chain services in the United Arab Emirates cover the commercial function of planning, moving, storing, clearing and coordinating goods across road, sea, air and multimodal networks. The function includes freight transport, freight forwarding, customs clearance, warehousing, bonded and free-zone storage, contract logistics, third-party logistics (3PL), distribution, transhipment and carrier coordination, together with the trade licence, customs code, permit and compliance layers that determine whether an operator may lawfully perform the selected activity.

The UAE is a federation of seven emirates, and logistics regulation operates through both federal and emirate-level structures. The correct route depends on the place of establishment, whether the undertaking is mainland or free-zone based, the relevant customs territory, transport mode, facility and trade lane. Federal customs policy is coordinated through the Federal Authority for Identity, Citizenship, Customs and Port Security and the UAE customs coordination framework, while Dubai Customs, Abu Dhabi Customs, Sharjah Customs and other emirate customs administrations process local customs operations. Goods released to UAE mainland are generally subject to the GCC Common Customs Law duty structure; goods entering a free zone generally have a different customs status until released to mainland.

Dubai has a distinct integrated logistics architecture: a UAE trade-licensed business registers with Dubai Customs and Dubai Trade, receives a Mirsal 2 Business Code and submits import, export, re-export, transit and free-zone declarations through Mirsal 2. Abu Dhabi has separate customs, economic-development and freight-transport structures; road freight activity is administered through Abu Dhabi Mobility's Asateel system, which requires permits and compliance with the limits of the permit before operations begin. Other emirates have their own business licensing, customs codes and local transport or port arrangements.

For international businesses, the UAE logistics environment should be assessed early alongside mainland/free-zone selection, emirate of incorporation, customs territory, port, airport or warehouse location, freight procurement, carrier and 3PL contracting, importer/exporter and declarant role, customs code and permits. A trade licence, customs client code, free-zone licence or freight permit does not replace other approvals. A change in emirate, free-zone/mainland model, goods category, warehouse status, fleet, route, customs procedure or operating model can require a fresh regulatory and operational assessment.

Object Definition

Logistics and supply chain services in the United Arab Emirates form the commercial function concerned with the planning, physical movement, storage, customs clearance and coordination of goods across domestic, GCC and international networks, and with determining which federal, emirate, mainland, free-zone, transport, customs and facility requirements apply to a given service role, goods category, location or trade lane. The function is broader than moving a shipment: it connects freight transport, forwarding, warehousing, bonded/free-zone status, contract logistics, 3PL coordination, customs codes, electronic declarations, transport permits, commercial documentation, liability rules and continuing regulatory supervision.

Purpose and Primary Outcome

The purpose of the logistics and supply chain function is to ensure that the movement, storage, forwarding, customs clearance, re-export and distribution of goods are conducted reliably, lawfully and to commercial expectations, by entities holding the trade licence, customs registration, transport permit, facility status and operational capacity required for the selected UAE model. The function establishes how goods flow through the supply chain, which party is responsible for each stage, and what emirate, federal, GCC, customs, VAT, reporting and liability obligations apply.

PurposeTo ensure reliable, lawful and properly documented planning, movement, storage, forwarding, customs clearance, re-export and distribution of goods within, into, out of or through the United Arab Emirates.
Primary OutcomeA functioning UAE logistics or supply chain arrangement — supported, where relevant, by an emirate mainland or free-zone trade licence, customs client/importer/exporter/agent code, customs declaration access, road freight permit, bonded or free-zone warehouse status, transit or re-export procedure and service agreement — that defines the operational and legal position of the importer, exporter, re-exporter, carrier, forwarder, clearing agent, warehouse operator, 3PL provider or trader.
Business ValueWell-structured logistics arrangements and early emirate/customs-territory clarity can reduce cargo delay, duty/VAT errors, free-zone release issues, contractual liability exposure, working-capital disruption and later enforcement or audit risk.

Logistics and supply chain work is normally activated by a new UAE trade lane, mainland or free-zone establishment, port, airport or warehouse requirement, re-export model, road freight operation or entry into import, export, forwarding or customs activity. The initial question is not simply whether a licence is required, but which emirate and jurisdictional route applies, whether goods are destined for mainland or free zone, who acts as importer, exporter, re-exporter, declarant, clearing agent, carrier, forwarder, warehouse operator or 3PL provider, and whether the entity's trade licence and customs status support the actual operating model.

Country Characteristics

The UAE logistics environment is shaped by a federal structure combined with highly developed emirate-level commercial, customs, transport and free-zone ecosystems. There is no single practical UAE operating route for all logistics functions. The question of whether an undertaking is mainland or free-zone based, and in which emirate it is established, can determine the competent licensing authority, customs client code, electronic declaration platform, warehouse status, import/re-export process and local road freight permit. Dubai and Abu Dhabi are particularly significant but should not be treated as interchangeable jurisdictions.

Operational CultureDigitally administered, trade-document intensive, internationally oriented and emirate/zone specific. Trade licence, Customs code, declaration, port, airport, free-zone, warehouse, carrier and client data must remain consistent across the relevant local and federal systems.
Institutional StructureFederal customs coordination operates alongside individual emirate customs administrations. Dubai Customs and Dubai Trade use Mirsal 2 for customs declarations. Abu Dhabi Customs administers local customs functions while Abu Dhabi Mobility operates Asateel for freight transport permits. Economic development departments and free-zone authorities issue the commercial trade licences on which customs and logistics roles are based.
Classification LogicThe initial classification distinguishes mainland, free zone, customs-bonded/controlled area and re-export/transit models; the next distinguishes the relevant emirate, trade licence activity, importer/exporter/declarant role, goods type, facility, transport mode and trade lane. Goods entering mainland are generally assessed under GCC customs duty arrangements, while free-zone goods have a different customs status until released to mainland.
Language ExpectationArabic is the official language and is central to government filings and legal instruments. English is extensively used in international logistics, ports, airports, free zones, customs platforms and commercial contracting. Formal submissions, trade licences, customs declarations and contracts should use the language, data format and terminology required by the specific emirate authority or free-zone administrator.

Key Authorities

The competent authority depends on the emirate, mainland/free-zone status, transport mode, goods type, facility and customs route. A single UAE supply chain can interact with federal customs coordination authorities, an emirate customs department, mainland economic development department, free-zone authority, road transport regulator, port or airport authority, clearing agent, warehouse/3PL provider and product-specific agency. Authority and system names should always be checked against the emirate in which the operation actually occurs.

Applicable Legislation

The UAE logistics function is governed by federal law, GCC customs principles, emirate customs procedures, emirate transport rules, mainland trade licensing and individual free-zone regulations. The substantive content of an individual operation cannot be reduced to a single federal licence: the relevant legal set depends on emirate, free-zone or mainland establishment, goods, transport mode, customs procedure, port/airport and trade lane. Freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics and 3PL activity are commercial functions but can connect to licensed activities, customs, facility, transport, goods-control and product-specific requirements.

Process Flow and Decision Tree

There is no single universal UAE logistics process because the appropriate route depends on emirate, mainland/free-zone status, service role, transport mode, goods, facility and trade lane. Nevertheless, most operations move from jurisdiction and operating-model classification into entity/trade licence setup, customs code and digital platform access, transport/facility preparation, customs declaration and clearance, delivery or re-export and ongoing compliance.

1. Define the Jurisdiction and Operating ModelIdentify the emirate, mainland or free-zone establishment, service role (importer, exporter, re-exporter, carrier, forwarder, clearing agent, warehouse, 3PL), transport mode, goods, facility, customs status and domestic/GCC/international trade lane.
2. Screen Legal and Customs TriggersAssess the trade licence activity, emirate freight permit, customs client code, importer/exporter/declarant role, mainland/free-zone customs status, duty/VAT position, transit/re-export model, controlled goods, warehouse/bonded status and other relevant regimes.
3. Identify the Competent Authority and PlatformDetermine the competent mainland economic department or free-zone authority, emirate customs department, transport regulator, port/airport authority and electronic platform. For example, Dubai uses Dubai Trade/Mirsal 2; Abu Dhabi road freight uses Asateel.
4. Establish Entity and Licensed ActivityIncorporate or register the mainland or free-zone entity, obtain the appropriate trade licence, confirm business activities, lease/approve the facility where relevant, identify authorised signatories and ensure that the legal establishment matches the planned logistics role.
5. Register for Customs and Transport AccessRegister with the competent emirate customs administration, obtain Customs Client/Business/Importer/Exporter/Agent Codes as applicable, activate digital declaration access, appoint a clearing agent if needed, and obtain transport or fleet permits where required.
6. Prepare Documentation and ContractsDevelop commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading/air waybill/CMR, tariff classification, Customs value, origin, trade licence, customs code, permits, warehouse/3PL agreement, freight forwarding agreement, insurance and responsibility matrix.
7. Lodge Declarations and Obtain ClearancesSubmit the appropriate emirate customs declaration for import, export, re-export, transit, free-zone or other procedure; obtain controlled-goods or product approvals; complete inspections, guarantees, duty/VAT and port/airport requirements.
8. Receive, Store, Distribute or Re-exportComplete Customs release or free-zone admission, move goods to the lawful warehouse/facility, maintain customs/inventory control, deliver to mainland, distribute within the UAE/GCC or re-export under the selected procedure.
9. Operate, Monitor and Manage ChangeMaintain trade licence, Customs code, electronic access, transport permits, facility approvals, declarations, duty/VAT, warehouse, goods-control and recordkeeping compliance; reassess before changing emirate, zone, goods, route, facility, service role or trade model.
Decision logic: First identify the emirate and whether the operation is mainland, free zone, bonded/controlled storage or a re-export/transit model. Then identify every party's legal role and the goods' customs status. Only after that classification can the correct trade licence, emirate customs code, declaration system, transport permit, facility status, documentation package and contractual responsibilities be planned reliably.

Timeline

UAE logistics setup, emirate establishment, customs registration, transport permits and facility approvals should be treated as part of supply-chain design rather than late administrative tasks. Timing depends on the emirate, mainland/free-zone model, trade licence activity, customs code registration, authorised-signatory access, digital platform, port/airport/free-zone facility, goods category, controlled-goods approvals and customs procedure. A business should not schedule cargo movement before the correct emirate customs registration and declaration route are active.

Cross-Border Relevance

Logistics and supply chain operations in the UAE are inherently cross-border. The country is a global port, air-cargo, free-zone, re-export and GCC trade jurisdiction. The UAE's federal-emirate structure adds a jurisdictional layer to the customs analysis: Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Sharjah and other emirates have separate customs administrations and digital procedures, while mainland/free-zone status changes the customs position of the goods. Foreign businesses must distinguish the UAE legal entity, local customs code, declarant/clearing agent, facility and trade licence from equivalent roles in the origin, destination and GCC jurisdictions.

Foreign CompaniesA foreign group intending to directly undertake UAE import/export, logistics or distribution activity should determine whether it needs a mainland entity, an emirate-specific free-zone entity, a local importer/exporter, a clearing agent, a licensed carrier, a warehouse/3PL provider or another authorised local participant. The trade licence, customs code, facility, contract and goods-control roles must align before operations start.
Federal and Emirate ContextThe UAE is a federation. Federal customs coordination and GCC customs principles do not eliminate the need to identify the competent emirate customs authority, trade licensing authority, digital declaration platform and local transport regulator. Dubai Mirsal 2 and Abu Dhabi customs/Asateel processes are specific examples of non-interchangeable local operating routes.
Mainland and Free Zone ContextGoods destined for UAE mainland are generally subject to the GCC common customs duty structure and applicable UAE VAT treatment. Goods entering a free zone generally have a different customs position until released to mainland. The commercial, Customs, inventory and contractual model should expressly state the goods' status and intended movement.
GCC and International ContextUAE operations frequently connect GCC land freight, Gulf ports, Asia-Europe sea lanes, African routes and global air cargo. A UAE customs declaration or free-zone arrangement does not itself resolve destination-country Customs, GCC border, transport permit, product-control, VAT or market-access requirements.
Customs Declaration ContextThe customs declarant must use the appropriate emirate customs registration and system. In Dubai, a person registered with Dubai Customs as authorised to import, export or transit goods may use Mirsal 2; brokers and consignees must maintain a valid Mirsal 2 Business Code. The relevant local process should be verified for each emirate.
Language ConsiderationsArabic is the official language and English is extensively used for international trade, ports, airports, free zones and commercial logistics. Cross-border documents must also meet the language, legalisation, certificate-of-origin, labelling, Customs and product-control requirements of origin and destination jurisdictions.
Practical RiskAssuming that a UAE free-zone licence permits mainland trading or that a customs code, declaration system or logistics licence issued in one emirate automatically authorises equivalent operations in another emirate, free zone or customs territory without separate confirmation.

Operating Constraints, Risks and Costs

The central practical risk is treating UAE logistics, trade licensing, customs, free-zone status and commercial contracts as separate administrative tasks rather than a single integrated operating model. Errors in emirate selection, mainland/free-zone classification, trade licence activity, importer/exporter/declarant role, customs client code, duty/VAT treatment, facility status or contract allocation can affect cargo release, cost, working capital and the ability to operate as planned.

Emirate and Customs Jurisdiction RiskA business may register with the wrong customs administration, assume that a Dubai, Abu Dhabi or other emirate code is transferable, or use a trade licence and facility structure that does not correspond to where the goods, customs clearance and transport activity actually occur.
Mainland and Free Zone RiskGoods moving between free zones and UAE mainland can have different duty, VAT, Customs declaration, inventory and release consequences. A free-zone entity should not assume that its licence, storage model or Customs position permits mainland distribution without the correct customs and commercial route.
Trade Licence and Activity RiskThe trade licence must cover the actual commercial activity. A company acting as carrier, freight forwarder, customs clearing agent, warehouse operator, 3PL provider, importer/exporter or trader may require distinct activity coverage, authority registrations, facility approvals or professional arrangements.
Customs Code and Declarant RiskThe importer/exporter, customs client code holder, declarant, clearing agent, broker, freight forwarder and warehouse provider can be different parties. Unclear authority, data ownership, customs representation, duty/VAT liability and document control can create clearance and contractual risk.
Transport Permit and Fleet RiskRoad freight permits, vehicle and driver rules, route restrictions, port/airport access and local freight regulations can vary by emirate. An Abu Dhabi freight permit under Asateel, for example, should not be assumed to substitute for other emirate requirements.
Goods Control and Customs Classification RiskIncorrect tariff classification, customs value, origin treatment, product description, controlled-goods licence, procedure selection or declaration data can affect duty, VAT, release timing, re-export eligibility, product compliance and customs audit exposure.

FAQ

Is there one UAE-wide customs registration for all emirates?No. Federal customs coordination exists, but customs operations are administered through emirate customs authorities. A business must identify the competent customs administration and its relevant registration, code and declaration system according to the emirate and customs route. Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Sharjah and other emirates have distinct operational procedures.
What is the difference between UAE mainland and a free zone for logistics?Mainland and free-zone structures have different commercial, customs and facility consequences. Goods destined for UAE mainland are generally subject to GCC customs duty and applicable UAE VAT treatment. Goods entering a free zone generally have a different customs status until released to mainland. The trade licence, customs declaration, warehouse and contract model must reflect the intended movement.
What is a Mirsal 2 Business Code in Dubai?It is the customs business identifier used in the Dubai Customs Mirsal 2 electronic declaration environment. A business registered with Dubai Customs as an authorised importer, exporter or transit party can use Mirsal 2, and brokers and consignees should maintain a valid Mirsal 2 Business Code for relevant services.
How does road freight regulation work in the UAE?Road freight regulation is materially emirate-specific. The relevant business must hold the appropriate trade licence and comply with local transport authority, vehicle, driver, route and operating conditions. In Abu Dhabi, freight transport is administered through Abu Dhabi Mobility's Asateel system and a permit must be obtained and observed before operations begin.
Can a UAE free-zone company import goods directly to mainland UAE customers?It can be commercially possible only through the appropriate customs and legal route. A free-zone licence and free-zone customs status do not automatically authorise mainland distribution. The business must assess the correct importer, customs declaration, duty/VAT, local trade licence, clearing agent, warehouse and delivery arrangement for the specific emirate and goods.
Are freight forwarding and warehousing separately regulated in the UAE?They can be. Freight forwarding, customs clearance, transport, warehousing, bonded storage, free-zone logistics and 3PL services are defined by the entity's emirate or free-zone trade licence, activity code, customs registration, facility approvals and transport or goods-specific rules. There is no single universal UAE logistics licence that replaces those separate requirements.

Operational Considerations

This section records the principal operational variables that commonly determine how a logistics or supply chain matter is classified, documented, examined and administered. The variables are registry-oriented reference points and do not determine the outcome of any individual case.

Jurisdiction and EstablishmentThe UAE emirate, mainland or free-zone entity, approved trade licence activity, registered address, authorised signatory, facility and actual operational location should be identified before transport, customs or warehouse arrangements are finalised.
Customs Territory and Goods StatusThe undertaking must identify whether goods are intended for UAE mainland, free zone, bonded/customs-controlled storage, transit, temporary admission, re-export or another customs procedure. This determines the competent emirate customs authority, declaration type, duty/VAT position, inventory controls and release model.
Service and Customs RoleThe distinction between importer, exporter, re-exporter, declarant, customs code holder, clearing agent, broker, carrier, freight forwarder, warehouse, bonded operator and 3PL provider determines the applicable trade licence, customs registration, authority, contractual and liability route.
Digital Declaration PlatformThe business must use the electronic customs and trade platform required by the competent emirate. Dubai uses Dubai Trade/Mirsal 2; Abu Dhabi and other emirates apply their respective customs platforms. Relevant user, business code, authorisation and clearing-agent access should be in place before cargo moves.
Transport and Facility ModelRoad freight, sea freight, air cargo, free-zone logistics, bonded storage, ordinary warehousing, fulfilment, contract logistics and 3PL models have different transport permit, customs, facility, inventory, security, port/airport and contractual consequences.
Evidence BaseTrade licence, constitutional/authorisation documents, customs code, digital platform access, invoices, packing lists, transport documents, tariff classification, value/origin evidence, permits, warehouse/3PL agreements, facility records and inventory data form the documentary basis where relevant.
Decision ScopeA trade licence, freight permit, customs client code, declaration, free-zone/bonded facility approval, customs procedure or service agreement may define the authorised activity, emirate, goods, route, facility, reporting duties, duty/VAT treatment, service level and other conditions. The scope should be read with the underlying authority and contractual material.
