Logistics and supply chain services in Mexico cover the commercial function of planning, moving, storing, clearing and coordinating goods across federal road, rail, sea, air and multimodal networks. The function includes federal motor freight, freight forwarding, customs brokerage, warehousing, contract logistics, third-party logistics (3PL), distribution, cross-border transport and carrier coordination, together with the permit, tax, customs, product-control and compliance layers that determine whether an operator may lawfully perform the selected activity.

Mexico's continental scale, federal structure, manufacturing and nearshoring supply chains, land borders with the United States and Guatemala, Pacific and Gulf/Caribbean ports, and North American trade integration make federal road freight, customs, warehousing and cross-border coordination central to the professional function. Federal cargo motor transport operating on federal roads requires a permit from the Secretariat of Infrastructure, Communications and Transport (SICT), through the Directorate-General for Federal Motor Transport (DGAF). The permit is tied to the carrier's RFC and specifies the service class, including general or specialised cargo, together with the authorised operating scope. International Mexico-US-Canada freight involves separate permanent-permit, vehicle, plate and route requirements.

For foreign trade, the Tax Administration Service (SAT) administers the Federal Taxpayer Registry (RFC), the Importers' Registry, sector-specific importers registry and electronic tax identity, while the National Customs Agency of Mexico (ANAM) administers customs clearance. The importer named on the pedimento — Mexico's official customs entry declaration — must generally be a legally constituted Mexican entity with active RFC and Importers' Registry status. VUCEM, the Digital Window for Mexican Foreign Trade, is the electronic single window through which Customs declarations, import/export applications and non-tariff regulatory documents are transmitted. The Electronic Value Manifest is required before the entry of goods under current customs procedures.

For international businesses, the Mexican logistics environment should be assessed early alongside Mexican entity and RFC structure, Importers' Registry status, border/port/airport/warehouse selection, SICT carrier licensing, customs broker appointment, VUCEM readiness, Carta Porte and CFDI documentation, product NOM compliance and USMCA/T-MEC origin strategy. A federal motor transport permit, RFC, Importers' Registry enrolment or pedimento does not replace other approvals. A change in fleet, freight class, importer of record, goods category, warehouse, trade lane, Customs procedure or operating model can require a fresh regulatory and operational assessment.

Core Outcome

A properly organised and lawfully compliant logistics or supply chain operation in Mexico — spanning federal road freight, customs clearance, freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, border, port or air-cargo distribution, multimodal transport or 3PL activity — supported by the relevant SICT permit, RFC and Importers' Registry status, VUCEM/pedimento procedures, Customs broker authority, product approvals and commercial contracts.

Object Definition

Logistics and supply chain services in Mexico form the commercial function concerned with the planning, physical movement, storage, Customs clearance and coordination of goods across domestic and international networks, and with determining which federal permits, tax registrations, Customs filings, product approvals and compliance measures apply to a given service role, vehicle, goods category, facility or trade lane. The function is broader than moving a shipment: it connects federal motor transport, freight forwarding, Customs brokerage, warehousing, contract logistics, 3PL coordination, RFC and Importers' Registry status, VUCEM declarations, pedimentos, Carta Porte, Customs valuation, origin, liability rules and continuing regulatory supervision.

DefinitionThe commercial and regulatory function covering logistics coordination, federal motor freight, freight forwarding, Customs brokerage, warehousing, contract logistics, 3PL services and Customs clearance for goods moving into, out of or within Mexico.

Purpose and Primary Outcome

The purpose of the logistics and supply chain function is to ensure that the movement, storage, forwarding and Customs clearance of goods are conducted reliably, lawfully and to commercial expectations, by entities holding the transport permits, Mexican tax/trade registrations, Customs status, product approvals and operational capacity required for the service model. The function establishes how goods flow through the supply chain, which party is responsible for each stage, and what Mexican transport, Customs, VAT, origin, reporting and liability obligations apply.

PurposeTo ensure reliable, lawful and properly documented planning, movement, storage, forwarding and Customs clearance of goods within, into, out of or through Mexico.
Primary OutcomeA functioning logistics or supply chain arrangement — supported, where relevant, by a SICT federal motor transport permit, RFC/e.firma and Importers' Registry status, VUCEM access, pedimento and Electronic Value Manifest, Customs broker authority, Carta Porte/CFDI, product approval, warehouse arrangement or service agreement — that defines the operational and legal position of the importer, exporter, carrier, forwarder, Customs broker, warehouse operator, 3PL provider or trader.
Business ValueWell-structured logistics arrangements and early regulatory clarity can reduce border delay, Customs and tax errors, product-control holds, contractual liability exposure, working-capital disruption and later enforcement or audit risk.

Request Contexts, Users and Scenarios

Logistics and supply chain work is normally activated by a new Mexico trade lane, federal road freight operation, USMCA border model, port, airport or warehouse requirement, or entry into import, export, forwarding or Customs activity. The initial question is not simply whether a permit is required, but whether the undertaking acts as carrier, importer of record, exporter, Customs broker, forwarder, warehouse operator, 3PL provider or trader, and whether its Mexican entity, RFC, Importers' Registry, VUCEM and SICT positions support the actual operating model.

Typical UserFederal motor carriers, freight forwarders, Customs brokers, importers of record, exporters, warehouse, fulfilment and 3PL operators, contract-logistics providers, border, port, airport and rail logistics participants, automotive/electronics/industrial manufacturers, e-commerce businesses, agricultural and food importers, investors and foreign companies operating in Mexico.
Business EventNew federal freight fleet, US-Mexico-Canada trade lane, border or inland warehouse establishment, 3PL contracting, SICT permit application, RFC/e.firma registration, Importers' Registry enrolment, VUCEM setup, Customs broker appointment, new sensitive product line, NOM conformity, IMMEX/warehouse arrangement or acquisition of a logistics business.
Typical ScenarioA carrier obtains a SICT federal cargo permit, federal plates and vehicle registration cards before operating on federal roads; a Mexican importer activates RFC, e.firma and Importers' Registry status, appoints a Customs broker and files a pedimento and Electronic Value Manifest through VUCEM; a US company structures a Mexican importer of record for cross-border manufacturing components; a manufacturer appoints a 3PL provider for Mexican warehousing and distribution and must allocate Customs, IVA, Carta Porte, inventory and delivery responsibilities.
Professional AssistanceTypically relevant when carrier, importer/exporter, Customs broker or warehouse roles are uncertain, SICT permit scope or cross-border access is complex, RFC/Importers' Registry/VUCEM status is not aligned, Customs valuation/classification/origin is difficult, product controls apply, or supply chain design spans multiple states, border points, ports, modes, providers and jurisdictions.

Country Characteristics

Mexico's logistics environment is shaped by its large territory, federal structure, USMCA integration, manufacturing and nearshoring clusters, long land border with the United States, Pacific and Gulf ports, domestic road freight dependence and a formal Customs/tax identity framework. The operating model must connect federal road transport permits with tax and Customs status. SICT regulates federal freight operation; SAT administers RFC, e.firma and importer registry status; ANAM administers Customs clearance; and VUCEM provides a central electronic route for foreign trade applications and non-tariff regulatory documents. The pedimento is central to the Customs process and identifies the legally responsible importing entity.

Operational CultureDocumentation-led, Customs/tax integrated, cross-border intensive and federal-state layered. Carrier, vehicle, RFC, e.firma, Importers' Registry, VUCEM, pedimento, Carta Porte, warehouse, port/airport/border and client data must remain consistent across Mexican systems.
Institutional StructureSICT/DGAF administers federal motor transport permits. SAT administers RFC, e.firma and the Importers' Registry. ANAM administers Customs clearance. VUCEM links Customs and non-tariff foreign trade procedures. State authorities, port/airport bodies, product regulators and Customs brokers remain material to the actual cargo operation.
Classification LogicThe first classification distinguishes federal versus non-federal road transport, domestic versus international freight, general versus specialised cargo and carrier versus owner/operator roles. The Customs classification then identifies the Mexican importer/exporter, RFC/e.firma, Importers' Registry, sectoral registry, pedimento, Customs broker, VUCEM, tariff, value, origin, product control and Customs procedure.
Language ExpectationSpanish is central to SICT, SAT, ANAM, VUCEM, Customs, state and legal procedures. English is widely used in USMCA and international logistics, but formal permits, Customs filings, invoices, value manifests, product documents and contracts should use precise Spanish terminology or professionally controlled translations.

The competent authority depends on service role, federal or local route, vehicle, goods, facility, port/airport/border and Customs procedure. A single Mexican supply chain operation may interact with SICT/DGAF, SAT, ANAM, VUCEM, state transport authorities, Customs brokers, port/airport/border Customs offices, warehouse/3PL providers and product-specific agencies because transport, Customs, tax, facility, goods and trade matters are administered through different channels.

Applicable Legislation

Mexico does not apply the EU Community Licence or Union Customs Code framework. Its logistics and supply chain function is governed by federal motor transport, Customs, foreign trade, tax, VAT, product-control and state-level transport law. The central classification questions are the actual business role, federal road use, vehicle and freight class, Mexican entity/RFC status, importer/exporter role, Importers' Registry enrolment, Customs broker authority, goods category, Customs procedure, facility and trade lane. Freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics and 3PL arrangements are commercial functions but frequently connect to regulated carrier, Customs, tax and product-control roles.

Process Flow and Decision Tree

There is no single universal Mexican logistics process because the correct route depends on service role, federal versus local transport, vehicle, state, goods, Customs status, product regulation, facility and trade lane. Nevertheless, most operations move from entity and supply-chain classification into federal permit and tax/trade registration, VUCEM and Customs preparation, facility and contract development, declaration and clearance, domestic or cross-border delivery and continuing compliance.

1. Define the OperationIdentify whether the undertaking acts as federal carrier, international carrier, freight forwarder, Customs broker, importer of record, exporter, warehouse operator, 3PL provider or trader; define vehicle, route, states, goods, facility, border/port/airport interface and domestic/international trade lane.
2. Screen Transport and Trade TriggersAssess federal motor transport permit, general/specialised cargo, international permit, vehicle plate/card, Carta Porte, RFC, e.firma, Importers' Registry, sectoral registry, VUCEM, Customs broker, pedimento, Electronic Value Manifest, tariff, value, origin, NOM, sanitary and other relevant requirements.
3. Identify the Competent Authority or CounterpartyDetermine whether the matter falls to SICT/DGAF, SAT, ANAM, VUCEM, state transport authority, Customs broker, port/airport/border Customs office, warehouse/3PL provider, Secretaría de Economía, SENASICA, COFEPRIS or another agency.
4. Establish Mexican Entity, RFC and Operating CapacityConfirm Mexican company, branch or authorised importer structure; obtain/verify RFC, e.firma, fiscal domicile, Buzón Tributario and tax compliance; establish carrier/forwarder/broker roles, vehicle access, warehouse facility, authorised signatories and commercial contracts.
5. Obtain Transport and Importer RegistrationsApply for SICT federal motor transport permit, plates and registration cards where the operation uses federal roads; enrol in the Importers' Registry and sectoral registry where required; establish VUCEM access and appoint a Customs broker or other authorised service provider.
6. Prepare Documentation and Goods ControlsDevelop permits, vehicle documents, Carta Porte/CFDI, commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading/air waybill/road document, tariff classification, Customs value, origin, Electronic Value Manifest, pedimento data, NOM/product-control approval and freight/warehouse agreements.
7. Lodge VUCEM and Customs DeclarationsTransmit required foreign trade information, Electronic Value Manifest and non-tariff documents through VUCEM; file the import or export pedimento through the authorised Customs/broker route; complete port, airport or border cargo, Customs and inspection processes.
8. Clearance, Delivery and ReconciliationComplete duties, IVA, Customs inspection, Customs clearance, product approval and cargo release; transport, store, distribute, export or re-export goods; reconcile RFC, VUCEM, pedimento, Carta Porte, Customs, tax, warehouse and commercial records.
9. Operate, Monitor and Manage ChangeMaintain SICT, RFC, Importers' Registry, VUCEM, Customs, tax, NOM/product, vehicle, Carta Porte, warehouse and recordkeeping compliance; reassess before changing vehicles, freight class, goods, facilities, states, importer/exporter roles or trade lanes.
Decision logic: First identify the Mexican entity, actual service role, federal road use, vehicle, goods and Customs/product status. Then determine whether SICT permit, RFC/e.firma, Importers' Registry, VUCEM, Customs broker, pedimento, Electronic Value Manifest, Carta Porte, NOM and warehouse routes apply. Only after that classification can the correct authority, document package, Customs sequence and contractual responsibilities be planned reliably.

Timeline

Mexican logistics setup, SICT carrier permits, RFC and Importers' Registry status, VUCEM access, Customs and product planning should be treated as part of supply-chain design rather than late administrative tasks. A Mexican importer should ensure that RFC, e.firma, fiscal domicile, tax compliance, Importers' Registry and, where relevant, sectoral registry status are active before cargo is scheduled. The Electronic Value Manifest must be transmitted through VUCEM before entry of goods, and Customs, border, port/airport, product and transport documents must be consistent with the party named on the pedimento.

Cross-Border Relevance

Logistics and supply chain operations in Mexico are inherently cross-border because Mexico is integrated into North American, Latin American and global manufacturing, trade, port and air-cargo networks. Mexico is outside the EU, EEA and EU Customs Union, so Mexican SICT permits, RFC, Importers' Registry, VUCEM and pedimento processes are distinct from EU Community Licence and EORI systems. The same goods flow can require Mexican importer registration, Customs broker authority, Customs and product clearance, federal road transport, USMCA/T-MEC origin evidence, and separate US, Canadian, Central American or other destination requirements.

Foreign CompaniesA foreign group intending to directly undertake Mexican import/export, logistics or distribution activity should determine whether a suitable Mexican entity, importer of record, RFC/e.firma holder, Importers' Registry member, SICT-licensed carrier, Customs broker, forwarder, warehouse or 3PL provider performs each regulated function. The entity, Customs, tax, product, facility, contract and goods-control roles must be aligned before operations start.
USMCA/T-MEC ContextMexico's North American trade integration makes origin, tariff classification, Customs value, carrier authority, border routing and product compliance material to many supply chains. A USMCA/T-MEC commercial claim should be supported by appropriate origin and Customs documentation and does not replace Mexican Importers' Registry, pedimento, Customs broker or product-control requirements.
Mexico-US-Canada Road Freight ContextInternational federal motor transport between Mexico, the United States and Canada requires the appropriate permit, plates, registration cards and operating authority. The legal, vehicle, carrier and Customs route should be analysed for the exact border zone, federal road, driver, vehicle configuration and commercial service model.
Customs and VUCEM ContextMexican Customs clearance depends on an importer/exporter with the correct RFC and registry status, the pedimento, Customs broker/representative route where used, VUCEM documents and the applicable Customs procedure. The Electronic Value Manifest must be transmitted before entry of goods under the current Customs framework.
Language ConsiderationsSpanish is central to Mexican SICT, SAT, ANAM, VUCEM, Customs, product control and contractual administration. English is widely used in North American logistics but formal permits, Customs entries, value manifests, product certificates and contracts should use precise Spanish terminology and meet Mexican legal requirements.
Practical RiskAssuming that a foreign importer of record, US or Canadian carrier authority, overseas Customs identifier, global 3PL agreement or USMCA/T-MEC origin claim automatically creates Mexican federal transport, RFC, Importers' Registry, VUCEM, Customs broker, pedimento, product-control or contractual authority without a separate Mexican operating-model assessment.

Operating Constraints, Risks and Costs

The central practical risk is treating Mexican logistics, federal carrier permits, RFC/Importers' Registry, VUCEM, Customs, product controls and commercial contracts as separate administrative tasks rather than a single integrated operating model. Errors in entity identity, carrier scope, importer status, Customs declaration, value/origin, product compliance, Carta Porte, warehouse arrangement or contract allocation can affect cargo release, cost, working capital and the ability to operate as planned.

Federal Permit and Operating Scope RiskA carrier may operate federal cargo transport without the required SICT permit, use an incorrect general/specialised or international service classification, or fail to align federal plates, vehicle registration cards, route and operating authority with the actual freight operation.
RFC and Importers' Registry RiskThe entity named as importer on the pedimento must generally have active RFC, valid e.firma, located fiscal domicile, active Buzón Tributario and Importers' Registry status. Failure in any of those elements can prevent or disrupt Customs clearance. Sensitive goods may require sector-specific registry membership.
VUCEM and Value Manifest RiskForeign trade documents, non-tariff approvals and the Electronic Value Manifest must be accurately transmitted through VUCEM. Inconsistent entity, value, origin, tariff, supplier, Customs broker or product data can delay entry, Customs clearance or audit reconciliation.
Pedimento, Customs Broker and Role RiskThe importer of record, RFC holder, Importers' Registry holder, Customs broker, pedimento declarant, carrier, forwarder, warehouse and 3PL provider may be different parties. Unclear authority, data ownership, Customs representation, duty/IVA liability and document custody can create clearance and contractual risk.
Product Control, NOM and Origin RiskIncorrect tariff classification, Customs value, origin, NOM conformity, sanitary/phytosanitary documents, sectoral registration or Customs procedure selection can affect duty, IVA, release timing, USMCA/T-MEC treatment, product compliance and Customs audit exposure.
Border, Port, State and Warehouse RiskBorder congestion, port/airport capacity, state road conditions, Customs inspection, storage, demurrage, Customs warehouse, IMMEX, inventory and Carta Porte requirements can create operational and contractual disruption if not planned in the route and service model.
Cost DriversSICT permits, federal plates and vehicle costs; RFC/Importers' Registry and compliance costs; Customs duties and IVA; Customs broker, VUCEM and electronic documentation costs; NOM/product certification; border, port, airport and terminal charges; warehouse and 3PL fees; tolls; professional advisers; and potential penalties, storage, detention or demurrage.

FAQ

What is a SICT federal cargo transport permit?It is the federal operating authority issued by SICT through the Directorate-General for Federal Motor Transport for a carrier to operate federal motor transport of cargo on federal roads and bridges. The permit is linked to the carrier's RFC and specifies the authorised service, such as general or specialised cargo, and the operating scope.
What is the difference between a federal cargo permit and an international freight permit?A federal cargo permit authorises the relevant Mexican federal motor transport service. International freight operations between Mexico and the United States or Canada require the separate applicable international permit, federal plates, registration cards and operating authority. The vehicle, route, border zone and service model determine the precise position.
What is the Importers' Registry in Mexico?The Importers' Registry is the SAT registry that allows a qualifying Mexican taxpayer to act as importer. The entity generally needs active RFC, valid e.firma, located fiscal domicile, active Buzón Tributario and compliant tax status. Sensitive goods can also require enrolment in the Sector-Specific Importers' Registry.
What is VUCEM?VUCEM is the Digital Window for Mexican Foreign Trade. It provides a single electronic entry point for applications and documents relating to imports, exports and transit, including non-tariff regulations and restrictions issued by participating government agencies. It is used for Customs-related foreign trade processes, including the Electronic Value Manifest.
What is a pedimento?A pedimento is the official Mexican Customs declaration used for import, export and other Customs procedures. It identifies the importer or exporter, tariff classification, Customs value, duties, taxes, regime and related data. It is ordinarily filed by an authorised customs broker or representative through the required electronic Customs/VUCEM process.
Are freight forwarding and warehousing separately licensed in Mexico?Freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics and 3PL are commercial functions, but the actual model can connect to distinct permits and approvals. Federal road cargo carriers require SICT authority; Customs brokers have their own Customs authorisation; Customs warehouses, IMMEX/maquiladora programmes, hazardous goods, controlled products, port/airport operations and specific facilities can require separate registrations, permits or compliance conditions.
