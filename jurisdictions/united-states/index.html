Logistics and supply chain services in the United States cover the commercial function of planning, moving, storing, clearing and coordinating goods across interstate road, rail, inland waterway, coastal and international sea, air and multimodal networks. The function includes motor carriage, freight brokerage, freight forwarding, customs brokerage, warehousing, contract logistics, third-party logistics (3PL), distribution, fulfilment, import/export operations and carrier coordination, together with the federal, state and local regulatory layers that determine whether an operator may lawfully perform the selected activity.

This federal record explains the United States as one national jurisdiction. It does not replace the separate state-level records that apply to state incorporation, intrastate motor carrier authority, commercial vehicle registration, fuel tax, sales/use tax, warehouse licensing, employment, zoning, environmental permits and local operations. At federal level, the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) regulates interstate motor carriers, freight brokers and freight forwarders. A USDOT Number identifies FMCSA-regulated operations; for-hire interstate motor carriers, brokers and freight forwarders generally require operating authority in addition to a USDOT Number. Operating authority is commonly recognised by its MC, FF or MX docket number.

For international trade, U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) administers imports, exports, cargo security, tariff classification, customs value, origin, customs bonds and customs procedures through the Automated Commercial Environment (ACE). ACE is the central U.S. system through which the trade community reports imports and exports and the government determines admissibility. Electronic import manifests for air, ocean, rail and truck modes must be filed in ACE. Importers or licensed customs brokers acting for them submit import declarations; exporters or their agents submit Electronic Export Information where required. The importer of record, customs broker, carrier, freight forwarder, consignee and warehouse roles must be distinguished clearly.

For international businesses, the U.S. logistics environment should be assessed early alongside entity and state-of-operation planning, interstate versus intrastate transport, freight broker/carrier/forwarder classification, federal and state vehicle compliance, ACE and customs bond arrangements, importer/exporter responsibility, product controls and warehouse/facility requirements. A USDOT Number, operating authority, ACE account or customs entry does not replace state or local approvals. A change in entity, vehicle fleet, interstate service, broker role, goods category, facility, trade lane or customs procedure can require a fresh federal, state and local assessment.

A properly organised and lawfully compliant U.S. logistics or supply chain operation — spanning interstate motor carriage, freight brokerage, freight forwarding, customs clearance, warehousing, contract logistics, fulfilment, port or air-cargo distribution, multimodal transport or 3PL activity — supported by the relevant federal registrations, operating authority, ACE/customs arrangements, state-level requirements and commercial contracts.

Object Definition

Logistics and supply chain services in the United States form the commercial function concerned with the planning, physical movement, storage, customs clearance and coordination of goods across domestic and international networks, and with determining which federal, state and local registrations, operating authorities, customs procedures and compliance measures apply to a given service role, vehicle, goods category, facility or trade lane. The function is broader than moving a shipment: it connects interstate motor carriage, freight brokerage, freight forwarding, customs brokerage, warehousing, fulfilment, contract logistics, 3PL coordination, USDOT and MC/FF/MX authority, ACE declarations, customs bonds, transport documentation, liability rules and continuing regulatory supervision.

Object Characteristics

These characteristics describe the general operating profile of logistics and supply chain services as a federal registry object in the United States. They are classificatory indicators rather than case-specific legal conclusions. The applicable state, local, federal, modal, goods and contract position must be assessed separately for an individual operation.

Market MaturityVery high. The United States has a large and mature logistics economy spanning interstate motor carriage, rail, inland waterways, ports, air cargo, ocean freight, freight brokerage, freight forwarding, customs brokerage, warehousing, fulfilment, contract logistics, 3PL, retail, manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, food, energy, technology and e-commerce supply chains.
Evidence StrengthHigh. Federal operating authority, customs entries, bonds, cargo manifests, state registrations, insurance filings, vehicle records, warehouse arrangements, goods classification, customs value, origin and commercial documents form the principal evidence base.
Standardisation LevelHigh at federal level for interstate motor carrier and Customs matters, particularly FMCSA USDOT/operating authority and CBP ACE processes. State and local rules remain material to intrastate carriage, entity formation, vehicle registration, taxes, land use, labour, warehouse operation and certain permits. This national record is therefore a federal baseline, not a substitute for the separate state records.
Cross-Border IntensityVery high. The United States has major land borders, international seaports, air cargo gateways and global trade links. Customs, ACE, customs bonds, North American trade, cargo security and importer/exporter roles are central to many supply chains.
Commercial ComplexityHigh. The distinction between motor carrier, broker, freight forwarder, customs broker, ocean transportation intermediary, importer of record, consignee, warehouse operator and 3PL provider; together with federal versus intrastate scope, state compliance, customs status and contractual liability, can affect operating authority, cargo release, insurance, cash flow and enforcement exposure.

The Registry Object covers the federal operational, registration, licensing and compliance architecture for commercial logistics and supply chain activity in the United States. It explains the national framework and identifies where state, territorial and local records are required. It focuses on interstate motor carrier authority, freight brokerage, freight forwarding, customs and ACE, warehousing and 3PL operations, multimodal interfaces and cross-border relevance.

Purpose and Primary Outcome

The purpose of the U.S. logistics and supply chain function is to ensure that interstate movement, storage, forwarding, brokerage, customs clearance and distribution of goods are conducted reliably, lawfully and to commercial expectations, by entities holding the federal registrations, operating authority, Customs status, state permissions and operational capacity required for the selected service model. The function establishes how goods flow through the supply chain, which party is responsible for each stage, and what federal, state, Customs, insurance, reporting and liability obligations apply.

PurposeTo ensure reliable, lawful and properly documented planning, movement, storage, forwarding, brokerage, customs clearance and distribution of goods within, into, out of or through the United States.
Primary OutcomeA functioning U.S. logistics or supply chain arrangement — supported, where relevant, by USDOT registration, MC/FF/MX operating authority, BOC-3 and insurance filings, ACE account and customs bond, import/export declarations, state/territory permits, warehouse arrangements and service agreements — that defines the operational and legal position of the carrier, broker, freight forwarder, customs broker, importer, exporter, warehouse operator, 3PL provider or trader.
Business ValueWell-structured logistics arrangements and early federal/state/customs clarity can reduce cargo delay, authority or insurance failures, Customs penalties, contractual liability exposure, working-capital disruption and later enforcement or audit risk.

Request Contexts, Users and Scenarios

Logistics and supply chain work is normally activated by a new U.S. entity, interstate freight model, carrier/broker/forwarder service, warehouse requirement, port or airport interface, import/export lane or state expansion. The initial question is not simply whether an MC number is needed, but whether the undertaking is a motor carrier, broker, freight forwarder, customs broker, importer of record, exporter, ocean transportation intermediary, warehouse operator, 3PL provider or trader, and whether the movement is interstate, intrastate, cross-border, exempt, regulated or goods-specific.

Typical UserInterstate motor carriers, owner-operators, freight brokers, freight forwarders, licensed customs brokers, importers of record, exporters, ocean transportation intermediaries, warehouse, fulfilment and 3PL operators, contract-logistics providers, rail, port and air-cargo participants, manufacturers, retailers, e-commerce businesses, food, pharmaceutical, industrial and technology companies, investors and foreign companies operating in the United States.
Business EventNew U.S. entity or state establishment, interstate fleet launch, MC/FF authority application, freight brokerage launch, Customs/ACE account setup, customs bond, port/airport warehouse establishment, 3PL contracting, import/export trade lane, USMCA cross-border operation, new regulated product line, state expansion or acquisition of a carrier/broker/forwarder.
Typical ScenarioA company begins hauling federally regulated goods owned by others in interstate commerce and obtains USDOT/MC authority, BOC-3 process-agent filing and required insurance; a freight broker registers for broker authority and bond; an importer of record establishes ACE access and a customs bond and appoints a licensed customs broker to file entries; a manufacturer appoints a 3PL for multi-state warehousing and distribution and must allocate federal carrier, state warehouse, Customs, tax, inventory and delivery responsibilities.
Professional AssistanceTypically relevant when carrier/broker/forwarder classification is uncertain, interstate versus intrastate authority is unclear, Customs valuation/classification/origin is complex, state requirements differ, a customs bond or special Customs procedure is needed, hazardous or controlled goods are involved, or the supply chain spans multiple states, ports, modes, providers and jurisdictions.

Country Characteristics

The United States logistics environment is shaped by a large federal system with substantial state, territorial and local operational authority. Interstate commerce is regulated at federal level, particularly through FMCSA for motor carriers, brokers and freight forwarders, CBP for Customs and ACE, FMC for ocean transportation intermediaries and other federal agencies for modal or goods-specific functions. However, state records remain essential to the network. A national operating authority does not eliminate state incorporation, intrastate carrier, vehicle, tax, warehouse, zoning, environmental, labour or local delivery requirements. This U.S. record should therefore be read as the federal parent record for subsequent state-level registry objects.

Operational CultureDocumentation-led, federally structured and state-dependent. USDOT/MC/FF authority, insurance, Customs, ACE, vehicle, warehouse, state tax, route, contract and client data must remain consistent across federal, state, local and counterpart-jurisdiction systems.
Institutional StructureFMCSA regulates interstate motor carriers, brokers and freight forwarders. CBP administers Customs and ACE. FMC regulates ocean transportation intermediaries. DOT, state departments of transportation, departments of motor vehicles, revenue agencies, port authorities, local zoning/fire bodies and goods-specific regulators govern adjacent functions.
Classification LogicThe initial classification distinguishes interstate from intrastate activity and identifies the business role: motor carrier, broker, freight forwarder, private carrier, customs broker, importer/exporter, OTI, warehouse or 3PL. The next classification identifies USDOT and operating authority requirements, Customs/ACE procedure, customs bond, state compliance, goods controls and facility model.
Language ExpectationEnglish is the primary working language for U.S. federal and state transport, Customs, commercial and legal documentation. Spanish and other languages can be commercially significant, but authoritative federal and state filings must follow the prescribed English-language forms, data fields and terminology.

Applicable Legislation

The United States does not operate under a single unitary logistics code. Interstate motor carrier and Customs functions are principally federal, while many transport, entity, tax, facility, employment and intrastate operations are governed by state or local law. The federal statutes and regulations below provide the national baseline. A specific operation must also be assessed against the law of every state, territory and local jurisdiction in which it is formed, based, registered, loaded, unloaded, warehoused or operated.

Process Flow and Decision Tree

There is no single universal U.S. logistics process because the correct route depends on the service role, interstate/intrastate scope, state of establishment, vehicle, goods category, facility and trade lane. Nevertheless, most operations move from entity and role classification into federal/state registration, operating authority or Customs preparation, facility and contract development, cargo movement or Customs declaration, and ongoing compliance.

1. Define the U.S. Operating ModelIdentify whether the undertaking acts as interstate motor carrier, private carrier, broker, freight forwarder, customs broker, ocean transportation intermediary, importer of record, exporter, warehouse operator, fulfilment provider, 3PL or trader; define goods, vehicles, states, facilities, ports/airports and domestic/international trade lanes.
2. Separate Federal and State QuestionsDetermine what is federal interstate/Customs activity and what is state, territorial or local activity. Assess the state of entity formation, headquarters, operating base, vehicle registration, intrastate movement, warehouse location, labour, tax, zoning and local permits separately.
3. Screen FMCSA AuthorityDetermine whether USDOT registration is required and whether the undertaking needs MC motor carrier, FF freight forwarder or broker operating authority. Identify BOC-3 process agent, insurance, safety, cargo, household goods and goods-specific requirements.
4. Establish Entity, Tax and Operating CapacityForm or register the U.S. entity in the applicable state; obtain EIN and state tax registrations; establish principal place of business, vehicles, drivers, management, insurance, process agent, broker/forwarder contracts, warehouse/facility rights and recordkeeping systems.
5. Obtain Federal and State RegistrationsApply through FMCSA Unified Registration System for USDOT and operating authority where required; make BOC-3 and insurance filings; obtain state intrastate permits, vehicle registration, IFTA/IRP, fuel tax, warehouse, fire, zoning and other state/local permissions as applicable.
6. Prepare Customs and ACE PositionIdentify importer of record, customs broker, ACE account, Importer Number, Customs bond, carrier/manifest filer, tariff classification, Customs value, origin, Partner Government Agency controls, entry type and export reporting requirements.
7. Prepare Documentation and ContractsDevelop bills of lading, rate confirmations, broker-carrier agreements, freight forwarding agreements, warehouse/3PL agreements, commercial invoices, packing lists, Customs entries, cargo manifests, bonds, insurance certificates, compliance manuals and responsibility matrices.
8. Launch Movement and ClearanceOperate only after relevant federal and state authority is active. File electronic manifests in ACE for all modes as required; lodge import entries or export declarations; complete duty, tax, Customs, PGA, port/airport, warehouse and delivery requirements.
9. Monitor, Update and Manage ChangeMaintain FMCSA, state, Customs, ACE, insurance, process-agent, vehicle, driver, warehouse, tax, contract and recordkeeping compliance. Reassess the position before changes in entity, authority, fleet, goods, routes, states, facilities, service role or trade lane.
Decision logic: First identify the actual service role and whether the movement is interstate, intrastate, cross-border or Customs-controlled. Then separate federal FMCSA/CBP/FMC requirements from the state, territorial and local requirements of each operating location. Only after that division is clear can the appropriate USDOT/authority, state permit, ACE/customs, facility, documentation and contractual route be planned reliably.

Timeline

U.S. logistics setup should be treated as a coordinated federal-state-customs programme rather than a late administrative step. A first-time applicant that has never received a USDOT Number registers through FMCSA's Unified Registration System. Operating authority is not active simply because an application is submitted: required insurance and BOC-3 process-agent filings must be in place and the authority must be granted before for-hire interstate regulated operations begin. Customs and ACE readiness, Customs bonds, state permits, warehouse approvals and product controls must be planned in parallel.

Early Planning StageDefine service role, interstate/intrastate scope, states of operation, vehicles, goods, port/airport/warehouse locations, Customs model, direct-versus-broker approach and federal/state compliance strategy.
Entity and State Setup StageForm/register the entity in the chosen state, obtain EIN and tax registrations, establish principal place of business, insurance, vehicle and driver arrangements, state permits, warehouse/facility rights, zoning/fire and local operating requirements.
FMCSA Registration StageObtain USDOT Number and, where required, MC/FF/MX operating authority through FMCSA. File BOC-3, ensure insurance filings are made and check Licensing and Insurance records until authority is granted and active.
Customs and ACE StageEstablish ACE access, importer/exporter roles, Customs broker authority, Customs bond, tariff/classification/value/origin data, cargo manifest responsibilities, Partner Government Agency controls and Customs procedure before the first shipment arrives or departs.
Documentation and Contract StagePrepare bills of lading, rate confirmations, broker/carrier/forwarder agreements, Customs and commercial documents, warehouse/3PL terms, insurance certificates, cargo claims procedures and regulatory recordkeeping.
Operational Launch StageBegin interstate transport only after authority, BOC-3, insurance and state registrations are active. File required ACE manifests and Customs entries; complete Customs/PGA release, warehouse receipt, distribution and delivery processes.
Ongoing Compliance and Change StageMaintain MCS-150, authority, insurance, state, IFTA/IRP, Customs/ACE, bond, vehicle, driver, warehouse and recordkeeping requirements. Reassess before changing entity, fleet, state footprint, goods, authority type, facility, Customs role or trade lane.

Cross-Border Relevance

Logistics and supply chain operations in the United States are inherently cross-border. The U.S. has major commercial interfaces with Canada, Mexico, the Caribbean, Latin America, Europe and Asia through land borders, seaports, airports, rail and inland networks. Federal motor carrier authority, CBP Customs and ACE are essential parts of the national framework, but cross-border movements also require country-specific analysis in the origin, destination and transit jurisdictions. The United States record therefore establishes the federal baseline; separate state records address domestic sub-national requirements, while country records address foreign jurisdictions.

Foreign CompaniesA foreign group intending to directly undertake U.S. logistics, import/export or distribution activity should determine whether a U.S. entity, foreign-qualified state entity, importer of record, FMCSA-registered carrier, broker, freight forwarder, licensed customs broker, warehouse or 3PL provider performs each regulated function. Federal authority, state registration, Customs, contract, tax and goods-control roles should be aligned before operations start.
Federal and State ContextFederal rules govern interstate commerce and Customs, but state and local rules govern many formation, intrastate, vehicle, tax, labour, facility, environmental and delivery matters. A federal USDOT Number, MC authority or ACE account does not remove state or local compliance duties. Each state should be reviewed through its own jurisdiction record.
Canada and Mexico ContextLand-border supply chains with Canada and Mexico involve FMCSA authority, CBP ACE manifests and Customs entries, USMCA origin analysis, Customs bonds, broker/carrier arrangements and the separate Canadian or Mexican transport, Customs, tax and product-control framework. A U.S. authority does not automatically provide authority in Canada or Mexico.
Customs and ACE ContextACE is the national electronic system through which imports and exports are reported and CBP determines admissibility. Electronic import manifests for air, ocean, rail and truck must be filed in ACE. The importer of record, carrier, broker, freight forwarder and Customs bond roles must be identified before cargo is loaded, manifested and entered.
State-Level Follow-On RecordsThis U.S. record intentionally preserves the federal overview. Subsequent state records should address state-specific incorporation and foreign qualification, intrastate motor carrier authority, commercial vehicle registration, IRP/IFTA, fuel and sales/use taxes, warehouse licensing, zoning, employment, environmental and local logistics requirements.
Language ConsiderationsEnglish is the primary language of U.S. federal and state logistics, Customs and legal administration. Cross-border goods flows may additionally require bilingual or foreign-language invoices, labels, certificates, origin documents and regulatory records in accordance with the requirements of Canada, Mexico or other counterpart jurisdictions.
Practical RiskAssuming that a federal FMCSA registration, MC number, ACE account, Customs bond, foreign carrier authority or global 3PL agreement alone authorises all state, local, Customs, product, tax, warehouse and international elements of the actual U.S. operating model.

Operating Constraints, Risks and Costs

The central practical risk is treating U.S. logistics, federal operating authority, state compliance, Customs and commercial contracts as separate administrative tasks rather than one integrated operating model. Errors in carrier/broker/forwarder classification, interstate/intrastate scope, entity authority, insurance, Customs role, state registration, product control, warehouse status or contract allocation can affect cargo movement, operating authority, release timing, cost and the ability to operate as planned.

Carrier, Broker and Forwarder Classification RiskA business may misclassify itself as a broker, freight forwarder, private carrier or 3PL while actually undertaking a different regulated role. For-hire interstate motor carriers, brokers and freight forwarders generally require operating authority in addition to a USDOT Number. The actual service, custody, tariff, transportation and payment model must be assessed.
Authority Activation RiskA USDOT Number or operating authority application does not itself authorise for-hire interstate operations. Required insurance and BOC-3 process-agent filings must be made and the authority must be granted. Authority status should be confirmed in FMCSA Licensing and Insurance before operations begin.
State and Intrastate RiskFederal interstate authority does not eliminate state/territory carrier permits, intrastate rules, vehicle registration, IRP/IFTA, fuel tax, sales/use tax, corporate qualification, warehouse/zoning/fire requirements or local delivery restrictions. The correct state record must be reviewed for each operating location.
Customs, ACE and Bond RiskIncorrect importer of record, Customs bond, ACE, cargo manifest, entry type, tariff classification, Customs value, origin, broker authority or Partner Government Agency data can delay release, increase duties/taxes, create penalties and expose the importer or responsible party to audit or enforcement action.
Goods and Product Control RiskCustoms clearance does not remove requirements imposed by FDA, USDA, EPA, CPSC, FCC, DOT hazardous materials rules, export controls, sanctions or other agencies. The goods, importer, carrier, forwarder, warehouse and broker roles must be aligned with the relevant product-control route.
Warehouse, Contract and Cargo Claims RiskUnclear allocation of title, custody, inventory control, Customs status, cargo claims, insurance, demurrage/detention, service levels, data ownership and indemnities between shipper, broker, carrier, forwarder, warehouse and 3PL provider can create significant operational and contractual exposure.
Cost DriversEntity and state registration, FMCSA application/authority, BOC-3, insurance, broker bond, vehicle/driver and IFTA/IRP compliance, Customs duties/taxes, Customs bond and broker fees, ACE/EDI systems, port/airport/rail charges, warehouse and 3PL fees, product controls, state taxes, professional advisers and potential penalties, storage, detention or demurrage.

FAQ

Does this United States record cover individual states?No. This is the federal parent record for the United States as a nation. It explains the national interstate motor carrier, Customs, ACE and federal logistics framework. Separate state records are required for state-specific entity formation, intrastate carrier authority, vehicle registration, fuel tax, sales/use tax, warehousing, zoning, labour, environmental and local operating requirements.
What is the difference between a USDOT Number and operating authority?A USDOT Number is the unique identifier FMCSA uses to identify an employer or person's operations. Operating authority is a separate FMCSA docket authority required for specified for-hire interstate motor carrier, broker and freight forwarder activities. It is commonly referred to as an MC, FF or MX number depending on the authority type.
Who generally needs interstate operating authority?In general, companies transporting federally regulated commodities owned by others for compensation in interstate commerce, or arranging their transport for compensation, need interstate operating authority in addition to a USDOT Number. For-hire interstate motor carriers, brokers and freight forwarders are the central categories. Exemptions and special categories should be assessed for the specific operation.
Can a USDOT or MC number be bought, leased or used by another company?No. FMCSA states that USDOT Numbers and operating authority belong to the assigned legal person and may not be sold, purchased, leased or rented for another party's use outside a legitimate corporate transaction. Using another entity's number can lead to inactivation or revocation.
What is ACE?ACE, the Automated Commercial Environment, is the United States' centralized digital system for processing imports and exports. It is the system through which the trade community reports imports and exports and CBP determines cargo admissibility. Electronic import manifests for air, ocean, rail and truck modes are filed in ACE.
Does an ACE account replace a customs broker or customs bond?No. ACE is the electronic system used for Customs reporting and processing. The importer of record remains responsible for entry information and Customs compliance. A licensed customs broker may file entries on the importer's behalf, and a Customs bond is required for many import transactions or Customs procedures. Each role and obligation should be assessed for the shipment and operating model.
Are warehousing and 3PL operations federally licensed in the same way as motor carriers?Not generally. Warehousing, fulfilment, contract logistics and many 3PL services are primarily organised by contract, state/local facility rules and the actual goods/service model. However, connected activities — such as interstate motor carriage, freight brokerage, freight forwarding, Customs brokerage, Customs warehousing, foreign-trade zones, hazardous goods, food, pharmaceuticals or environmental operations — can require separate federal, state or local permissions and compliance.

Operational Considerations

This section records the principal operational variables that commonly determine how a logistics or supply chain matter is classified, documented, examined and administered. The variables are registry-oriented reference points and do not determine the outcome of any individual case.

Federal and State LayeringThe U.S. federal record establishes interstate motor carrier, Customs and national transport principles. Every operation must also identify the relevant states, territories and local jurisdictions for entity registration, intrastate transport, vehicles, taxes, facilities, warehouses, employment, zoning and local rules. This is the core reason state-level registry records follow this national record.
Service Role ClassificationThe distinction between motor carrier, private carrier, broker, freight forwarder, customs broker, ocean transportation intermediary, importer of record, exporter, warehouse operator and 3PL provider determines the applicable FMCSA, CBP, FMC, state, insurance, bond and contractual route.
Interstate and Intrastate ScopeThe undertaking should determine whether its transport is interstate commerce, intrastate movement, cross-border, exempt carriage, private carriage or for-hire regulated carriage. Federal authority and state authority can apply differently to the same commercial network depending on the actual service and movement.
Customs and Trade RouteImporter of record, ACE account, Customs bond, broker authority, carrier/manifest filer, tariff classification, Customs value, origin, entry type, PGA controls, export filing and counterpart-jurisdiction requirements should be established before cargo is loaded, manifested or entered.
Facility and Storage ModelOrdinary warehousing, fulfilment, contract logistics, 3PL, Customs warehouse, foreign-trade zone, bonded storage, port/airport storage and hazardous/product-controlled storage have different federal, state, local, Customs, tax, inventory, security and contractual consequences.
Evidence BaseEntity and state documents, USDOT/MC/FF/MX authority, BOC-3, insurance, bonds, vehicle/driver records, ACE data, manifests, Customs entries, invoices, packing lists, bills of lading, tariff/value/origin evidence, state permits, warehouse agreements and inventory records form the documentary basis where relevant.
Decision ScopeA federal operating authority, state permit, ACE transaction, Customs bond, Customs procedure, warehouse/FTZ approval or service agreement may define the legal entity, service, route, vehicle, goods, facility, reporting duties, Customs status, insurance, service levels and other conditions. The scope should be read with the underlying application, filing and contractual material.
Change ManagementLater changes in entity, state footprint, principal office, service role, fleet, vehicles, drivers, goods category, hazardous status, trade lane, importer/exporter/broker role, Customs procedure, warehouse model, facility, ownership, authority or operating assumptions may require renewed assessment against federal, state, Customs, tax, insurance and contractual requirements.
