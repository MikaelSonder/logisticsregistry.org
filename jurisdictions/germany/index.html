Logistics and supply chain services in Germany cover the commercial function of planning, moving, storing, clearing and coordinating goods across road, rail, inland waterway, sea, air and multimodal networks. The function includes freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, third-party logistics (3PL), distribution, customs handling and carrier coordination, together with the licensing and compliance layers that determine whether an operator may lawfully perform regulated transport activity.

Germany's central position in Europe, dense motorway and rail networks, inland waterways, major seaports and large industrial, retail and e-commerce supply chains make it a key freight and logistics jurisdiction. Commercial road haulage is regulated through national law and directly applicable EU rules. Licensing is generally handled by the locally competent authority at the company's place of business, while the Federal Office for Logistics and Mobility (BALM) has federal functions including own-account freight registration and permits for specified third-country road-haulage routes. A national road-haulage permit applies to relevant domestic commercial activity, while a Community Licence supports eligible international EU/EEA operations.

For trade with countries outside the EU, the German customs administration (Zoll) administers import, export, transit, tariff classification, customs authorisations and EORI identification through the EU customs framework. The EORI number is valid throughout the European Union, replaces the former German customs number and is issued free of charge by the General Directorate of Customs. EORI applications are made through the Customs Portal; the transitional paper route using Form 0870a ends on 30 September 2026.

For international businesses, the German logistics environment should be assessed early alongside supply chain design, warehouse location, freight procurement, carrier and 3PL contracting, customs strategy, German establishment requirements and EU single-market obligations. A transport permit, Community Licence or customs authorisation does not replace other approvals, and a change in fleet, vehicle class, goods category, trade lane or operating model can require a fresh regulatory and operational assessment.

Core Outcome

A properly organised and lawfully compliant logistics or supply chain operation in Germany — spanning transport, customs clearance, warehousing, freight forwarding, contract logistics, multimodal distribution or 3PL activity — supported by the relevant permits, licences, registrations, authorisations and commercial arrangements.

Object Definition

Logistics and supply chain services in Germany form the commercial function concerned with the planning, physical movement, storage, customs clearance and coordination of goods for hire or reward, and with determining which licences, registrations and compliance measures apply to a given transport mode, service type, trade lane or goods category. The function is broader than moving a shipment: it connects freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, 3PL coordination, multimodal planning, carrier licensing, customs classification, transport documentation, liability rules and ongoing regulatory supervision.

DefinitionThe commercial and regulatory function covering logistics coordination, freight transport, freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, 3PL services and customs clearance for goods moving into, out of or within Germany.

Object Characteristics

These characteristics describe the general operating profile of logistics and supply chain services as a registry object in Germany. They are classificatory indicators rather than case-specific legal conclusions; the profile of an individual matter will vary with the service type, transport mode, goods category, trade lane and applicable licensing or customs route.

Market MaturityHigh. Germany has a large, established logistics market spanning industrial transport, contract logistics, forwarding, warehousing, retail distribution, e-commerce and multimodal freight services.
Evidence StrengthHigh. Road-haulage permits and customs decisions are normally based on documented establishment, good repute, financial standing, professional competence, corporate registration and transport or trade documentation.
Standardisation LevelHigh. EU rules on road-transport market access and the Union Customs Code create a substantially harmonised framework, while forwarding, warehousing and 3PL activity are primarily organised through commercial contracts and industry terms.
Cross-Border IntensityHigh. Germany's central EU location and extensive connections with neighbouring states, global ports and international freight corridors make cross-border road, rail, sea and air operations central to the function.
Commercial ComplexityHigh. Permit scope, cabotage and driver requirements, customs classification, contract and liability allocation, infrastructure constraints and multimodal interfaces can affect delivery timing, landed cost, working capital and compliance risk.

Scope

The Registry Object covers the practical operational, licensing and compliance architecture for commercial logistics and supply chain activity in Germany. It focuses on the early classification question, the competent authority where regulation applies, the information base, the operational process and the practical consequences of a haulage permit, Community Licence, customs authorisation, warehouse arrangement or freight-forwarding engagement.

Covered MattersLogistics coordination and supply chain planning; freight forwarding; warehousing, contract logistics and 3PL services; road, rail, inland-waterway, sea and air freight; national road-haulage permits; Community Licences and cabotage; EORI and customs declarations; tariff classification; ATLAS customs systems; authorised economic operator status; bonded storage and third-country permits.
Functional BoundaryThe object explains logistics and supply chain operation and regulation as a commercial and administrative process. It does not replace project-specific legal, tax, customs brokerage, employment, competition, maritime or supply chain engineering advice.
Related but Not PrimaryVehicle roadworthiness, driver working-time and posting rules, minimum-wage obligations, dangerous-goods certification, oversized-load permits, port and aviation safety, environmental permits for depots, sanctions and product-specific import restrictions may be connected but have separate legal routes.
Outside ScopeGeneric supply chain consulting unrelated to a defined operational or regulatory question, voluntary sustainability programmes and general commercial freight-rate negotiation without direct logistics, licensing or customs relevance.

Purpose and Primary Outcome

The purpose of the logistics and supply chain function is to ensure that the movement, storage, forwarding and customs clearance of goods are conducted reliably, lawfully and to commercial expectations, by operators who meet applicable licensing, financial and professional standards where regulation applies. The function establishes how goods flow through the supply chain, which party is responsible for each stage, and what documentation, reporting and liability obligations apply.

PurposeTo ensure reliable, lawful and properly documented planning, movement, storage, forwarding and customs clearance of goods within, into and out of Germany.
Primary OutcomeA functioning logistics or supply chain arrangement — supported, where relevant, by a national road-haulage permit, Community Licence, third-country permit, EORI registration, customs authorisation or warehousing agreement — that defines the operational and legal position of the carrier, forwarder, warehouse operator, 3PL provider or trader.
Business ValueWell-structured logistics arrangements and early regulatory clarity can reduce shipment delay, customs penalties, contractual liability exposure, supply-chain disruption and later enforcement or audit risk.

Request Contexts, Users and Scenarios

Logistics and supply chain work is normally activated by a new trade lane, fleet expansion, warehouse requirement or entry into cross-border freight, forwarding or customs activity. The initial question is not simply whether a permit is required, but how the goods flow should be organised, whether the proposed activity falls within a regulated transport or customs category, and whether it changes the legal scope of an existing authorisation or commercial arrangement.

Typical UserRoad-haulage operators, freight forwarders, customs brokers, warehouse and 3PL operators, contract-logistics providers, rail and inland-waterway logistics participants, manufacturers with in-house logistics, e-commerce importers, investors and foreign companies establishing German freight or distribution operations.
Business EventNew haulage fleet, cross-border trade lane, warehouse or fulfilment-centre establishment, 3PL contracting, haulage permit or Community Licence application, acquisition of a licensed carrier, new import/export product line, third-country route or cabotage expansion.
Typical ScenarioA company plans to operate road freight services from Germany and must decide between a national permit and Community Licence; an importer must obtain an EORI number before using German customs systems; a manufacturer appoints a 3PL provider and allocates warehouse, customs and delivery responsibilities; a forwarder designs a rail-road-port route across several EU states.
Professional AssistanceTypically relevant when permit status is uncertain, customs classification is complex, supply chain design spans multiple carriers and jurisdictions, driver or cabotage rules apply, or the operation involves third-country trade.

Country Characteristics

Germany's logistics environment is shaped by its central European position, large domestic economy, dense road and rail corridors, Rhine and other inland waterways, North Sea and Baltic ports, EU Customs Union membership and a federal administrative structure. Licensing for commercial road haulage is generally managed by the locally competent authority at the carrier's place of business, whereas federal institutions retain specialised transport and customs functions. This decentralised licensing structure is an important operational feature for companies establishing German transport operations.

Operational CultureDocumentation-led, technically structured and institutionally distributed. Permit, customs, corporate and transport records must remain consistent across local authority, federal and EU compliance layers.
Institutional StructureCommercial road-haulage permits and Community Licences are issued by locally competent authorities. BALM carries specialised federal functions, including own-account freight registration and specified third-country permits. Zoll and the General Directorate of Customs administer customs, EORI and ATLAS-related functions.
Classification LogicCommercial goods transport above the relevant weight threshold requires a national permit or Community Licence. International operations using motor vehicles above 2.5 tonnes require a Community Licence under the applicable EU rules. Own-account transport may be exempt where the statutory criteria are met but can entail registration duties.
Language ExpectationGerman is central to local licensing and administrative correspondence. German Customs provides English-language guidance for key trader services, while formal applications and local authority documentation should use precise German terminology.

A single German supply chain operation may interact with local licensing authorities, BALM, customs offices, the General Directorate of Customs, port or infrastructure authorities and sectoral regulators because licensing, customs, route, vehicle and warehouse-related matters are administered through different channels.

Applicable Legislation

EU Regulations 1071/2009 and 1072/2009 form the principal framework for access to the road-haulage profession and the international road-haulage market, while the Union Customs Code governs customs treatment across the EU, including Germany. Germany's Road Haulage Act (GüKG) provides the domestic framework for commercial goods transport and distinguishes commercial haulage from own-account transport. Freight forwarding, warehousing and contract logistics are generally organised through commercial contract and general German commercial law, unless a connected activity is separately regulated.

Process Flow and Decision Tree

There is no single universal logistics process because the appropriate route depends on service type, transport mode, vehicle category, company location and trade lane. Nevertheless, most German operations move from planning and classification into establishment and registration, preparation of operational and financial evidence, formal licensing, customs or contracting setup, review, decision and ongoing compliance with permit conditions, customs obligations or service agreements.

1. Define the OperationIdentify the service type (transport, forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, 3PL), transport mode, vehicle class, goods category, trade lane and whether movement is domestic, intra-EU or third-country.
2. Screen Legal TriggersAssess the activity against GüKG, EU Community Licence rules, own-account transport criteria, third-country permit requirements, the Union Customs Code and other relevant regimes.
3. Identify the Competent Authority or CounterpartyDetermine whether the matter falls to the locally competent licensing authority, BALM, Zoll, GZD, a port or infrastructure authority, a warehouse or 3PL provider, or another party.
4. Register and Prepare EvidenceConfirm German company establishment and, where relevant, own-account transport registration; obtain EORI identification and assemble good-repute, professional-competence, financial-standing and corporate evidence.
5. Prepare the Documentation BaseDevelop permit applications, transport and warehousing contracts, tariff-classification records, customs declarations, third-country permit material and proposed operating conditions.
6. Submit, Contract and CompleteFile the national permit, Community Licence, EORI, customs or third-country permit request, or finalise the forwarding or warehousing agreement; address requests for clarification or supplementary material.
7. ExaminationThe authority or counterparty reviews the material, may request further evidence, and assesses establishment, good repute, financial standing, professional competence, classification accuracy or service terms.
8. Decision and ConditionsA permit, Community Licence, EORI confirmation, customs authorisation, third-country permit or service agreement may set scope, validity, reporting duties, cabotage limits and other operating conditions.
9. Operate, Monitor and Manage ChangeMaintain compliance, meet reporting, registration and customs duties, and assess whether fleet, service scope, trade lane or goods-category changes require renewal, amendment or a new process.
Decision logic: First identify the service type, transport mode, company location and trade lane. Then determine whether commercial or own-account transport rules apply and identify the relevant licensing, customs and contractual regime. Only after the operational route is identified can the competent authority or provider, documentation package and compliance timeline be reliably planned.

Timeline

Logistics setup, road-haulage licensing and customs registration should be treated as part of supply chain planning rather than a late administrative step. Timing depends on the completeness of corporate, conduct-history, financial and professional material, the processing capacity of the locally competent authority, any federal permit requirement, customs complexity and the goods category involved. EORI should be secured before it is needed in customs declarations.

Early Planning StageTrade-lane definition, transport mode and service selection, commercial versus own-account assessment, legal classification and licensing, customs or contracting strategy.
Pre-Application StageGerman-establishment confirmation, certificates of good conduct, commercial-register data, financial-standing and professional-competence evidence, EORI preparation and customs-document collection.
Submission StageThe national permit or Community Licence application is lodged with the local authority; EORI and customs services are handled through the Customs Portal; relevant federal permit applications are made to BALM; forwarding or warehousing agreements are finalised.
Examination StageCompleteness review, verification of good repute, financial standing and professional competence, and possible requests for clarification or additional material.
Decision StageNational road-haulage permit, Community Licence, EORI confirmation, customs authorisation, third-country permit, signed service agreement, refusal or other formal result.
Post-Decision StageImplementation, licence-document control, customs-declaration testing, licence-display compliance and ongoing operational management.
Change StageBefore fleet expansion, new trade lanes, service-scope changes, new goods categories or a corporate restructuring, reassess existing permits, licences, customs procedures, registrations and agreements.

Cross-Border Relevance

Logistics and supply chain operations in Germany are inherently cross-border. Germany's central position connects it directly to nine neighbouring states and to major European road, rail, inland-waterway, sea and air freight corridors. It participates in the EU single market and Customs Union, so EU road-haulage and customs rules are central; trade with third countries requires the appropriate customs and, where relevant, transport-permit planning. Foreign investors, group companies, carriers, forwarders, warehouse operators, customs representatives and 3PL providers need clarity on which entity holds the licence, acts as importer or exporter and bears customs and contractual responsibilities.

Foreign CompaniesA foreign-owned undertaking established in Germany is generally assessed under the same German and EU framework as a domestic operator. The competent haulage licensing authority is normally determined by the place of business, and a genuine local operating establishment is material to the licence analysis.
EU ContextA German Community Licence supports eligible international road haulage throughout the EU/EEA, subject to cabotage, driver, vehicle and other applicable rules. It is distinct from a national German road-haulage permit.
Third-Country ContextRoad haulage to or through countries outside the EU/EEA, Switzerland or the United Kingdom can require bilateral or CEMT permits in addition to the regulatory position needed for the German part of the route. Trade with third countries also requires EU customs procedures.
Customs ContextGermany is part of the EU Customs Union. The German EORI number is valid throughout the EU and is used to identify economic operators in customs processes. Goods entering or leaving the EU customs territory require the applicable customs procedure and supporting documentation.
Language ConsiderationsGerman is central to local licensing and authority interaction. German Customs offers English-language guidance for important trader processes, but formal applications, licences and contracts should use terminology appropriate to the authority and commercial relationship.
Practical RiskAssuming that a Community Licence resolves domestic German permit questions, third-country permit requirements or customs obligations, or that a foreign establishment and authorisation automatically satisfies German licensing requirements without separate assessment.

Operating Constraints, Risks and Costs

The central practical risk is treating German logistics licensing and customs compliance as a one-time filing exercise rather than an ongoing operational and compliance process. Permit classification errors, incomplete conduct-history or financial evidence, inconsistent trade or service documentation and insufficient attention to federal, local and EU conditions can affect timing, cost and the ability to operate as planned.

Permit Classification RiskThe undertaking may incorrectly treat commercial transport as own-account transport, use a national permit where a Community Licence is required, or overlook third-country permit needs.
Local Authority RiskPermit applications are generally processed by the local authority responsible for the company's place of business. The appropriate authority, procedure and documentary expectations must therefore be identified at an early stage.
Good Repute and Evidence RiskCertificates of good conduct, commercial-register information, financial-standing evidence and professional competence documentation are central to permit assessment; incomplete or outdated evidence can delay the application.
Customs Identity RiskImport, export and customs-procedure activity can be delayed where the trader, declarant or representative lacks an appropriate EORI identity or consistent master data in the customs system.
Change and Cabotage RiskFleet expansion, new trade lanes, changed service scope, cabotage activity, driver arrangements or new goods categories may require permit, customs, employment or contractual reassessment.
Cost DriversPermit and certificate fees, financial-standing capital, customs duties and import VAT, tolls, port and warehouse charges, 3PL service fees, ATLAS and transport-management systems, professional advisers and possible penalty exposure.

FAQ

When is a German road-haulage permit or Community Licence required?Commercial goods transport with vehicles above 3.5 tonnes generally requires a permit. A national permit is valid only in Germany, while a Community Licence is required for eligible international road-haulage operations and is valid within the European Union. Cross-border transport with vehicles above 2.5 tonnes requires a Community Licence under the applicable EU rules.
Who issues road-haulage permits in Germany?The locally competent licensing authority at the undertaking's place of business generally issues the national permit or Community Licence. BALM has separate federal functions, including own-account freight registration and certain third-country or CEMT permits.
Does own-account transport require a permit in Germany?Own-account transport may be exempt from the commercial road-haulage permit requirement where the statutory conditions are met. Companies carrying out eligible own-account road haulage must nevertheless register the activity with BALM before the first transport operation.
What is a German EORI number?The EORI number is the EU-wide identification number for economic operators involved in customs activities. It replaces the former German customs number and is issued free of charge by the General Directorate of Customs through the Customs Portal.
Are freight forwarding and warehousing separately licensed in Germany?Freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics and 3PL services are generally organised through commercial contract and general German commercial law rather than a dedicated professional licence. Connected activities such as commercial road haulage, customs warehousing, customs representation or dangerous-goods handling may carry separate requirements.
When are third-country road-haulage permits needed?For commercial road haulage to or through countries outside the EU/EEA, Switzerland or the United Kingdom, bilateral or CEMT permits can be required in addition to the Community Licence for the relevant route sections. BALM is responsible for these permit functions in Germany.
