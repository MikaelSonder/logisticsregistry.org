Logistics and supply chain services in Switzerland cover the commercial function of planning, moving, storing, clearing and coordinating goods across road, rail, sea-linked, air and multimodal networks. The function includes freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, third-party logistics (3PL), distribution, customs handling and carrier coordination, together with the licensing and compliance layers that determine whether an operator may lawfully perform regulated transport activity.

Switzerland's landlocked central-European position, Alpine geography, high-value export economy, major north-south freight corridors and proximity to Germany, France, Italy, Austria and Liechtenstein make road, rail, intermodal and customs management central to the professional function. Professional road transport companies require a licence from the Federal Office of Transport (FOT/OFT), valid for five years. From 1 May 2025, professional goods transport with vehicles exceeding 2.5 tonnes is generally subject to licensing. An exception applies to domestic Swiss professional goods carriage exclusively using vehicles over 2.5 tonnes but not exceeding 3.5 tonnes. Operators must satisfy good repute, financial capacity, professional capacity and a real and durable Swiss establishment.

Switzerland is neither an EU member nor part of the EU Customs Union. Customs therefore remains a core operational function for most Switzerland-EU goods movements. The Federal Office for Customs and Border Security (FOCBS/BAZG) uses the Swiss UID, not an EU EORI number, to identify companies in Swiss electronic customs declarations. Every company operating in Switzerland receives a UID and needs it to declare goods electronically in Swiss customs systems. EORI is relevant when a Swiss operator interacts directly with EU customs authorities, for example for EU electronic pre-notification.

For international businesses, the Swiss logistics environment should be assessed early alongside supply chain design, Alpine corridor and terminal selection, freight procurement, carrier and 3PL contracting, customs strategy, Swiss establishment and EU-border interfaces. A FOT licence, UID or customs arrangement does not replace other approvals, and a change in fleet, vehicle category, goods type, trade lane, customs procedure or operating model can require a fresh regulatory and operational assessment.

Core Outcome

A properly organised and lawfully compliant logistics or supply chain operation in Switzerland — spanning transport, customs clearance, warehousing, freight forwarding, contract logistics, Alpine or rail-linked distribution, multimodal transport or 3PL activity — supported by the relevant FOT licence, UID, customs arrangements, international permits and commercial contracts.

Object Definition

Logistics and supply chain services in Switzerland form the commercial function concerned with the planning, physical movement, storage, customs clearance and coordination of goods for hire or reward, and with determining which licences, registrations and compliance measures apply to a given transport mode, service type, trade lane or goods category. The function is broader than moving a shipment: it connects freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, 3PL coordination, Alpine and rail intermodal planning, carrier licensing, Swiss customs status, EU EORI interface, transport documentation, liability rules and ongoing regulatory supervision.

DefinitionThe commercial and regulatory function covering logistics coordination, freight transport, freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics, 3PL services and customs clearance for goods moving into, out of or within Switzerland.

The Registry Object covers the practical operational, licensing and compliance architecture for commercial logistics and supply chain activity in Switzerland. It focuses on the early classification question, the competent authority where regulation applies, the information base, the operational process and the practical consequences of a FOT licence, Swiss customs procedure, UID registration, warehouse arrangement or freight-forwarding engagement.

Purpose and Primary Outcome

The purpose of the logistics and supply chain function is to ensure that the movement, storage, forwarding and customs clearance of goods are conducted reliably, lawfully and to commercial expectations, by operators who meet applicable establishment, repute, financial and professional standards where regulation applies. The function establishes how goods flow through the supply chain, which party is responsible for each stage, and what Swiss and EU customs, reporting and liability obligations apply.

PurposeTo ensure reliable, lawful and properly documented planning, movement, storage, forwarding and customs clearance of goods within, into and out of Switzerland.
Primary OutcomeA functioning logistics or supply chain arrangement — supported, where relevant, by a FOT road transport licence, Swiss UID, EU EORI interface, customs authorisation, transit arrangement, international permit or warehousing agreement — that defines the operational and legal position of the carrier, forwarder, warehouse operator, contract-logistics or 3PL provider, or trader.
Business ValueWell-structured logistics arrangements and early regulatory clarity can reduce border delay, customs penalties, route disruption, contractual liability exposure and later enforcement or audit risk.

Request Contexts, Users and Scenarios

Logistics and supply chain work is normally activated by a new Swiss trade lane, fleet expansion, terminal or warehouse requirement, or entry into cross-border freight, forwarding or customs activity. The initial question is not simply whether a licence is required, but how the goods flow should be organised across a Swiss-EU customs border, whether the planned vehicles fall within the FOT licence regime, whether the activity falls within a regulated transport or customs category, and whether it changes the legal scope of an existing authorisation or commercial arrangement.

Typical UserRoad-haulage operators, freight forwarders, customs brokers, warehouse, fulfilment and 3PL operators, contract-logistics providers, rail and Alpine logistics participants, manufacturers with in-house logistics, high-value goods and pharmaceutical supply-chain participants, e-commerce importers, investors and foreign companies establishing Swiss freight or distribution operations.
Business EventNew haulage fleet, Swiss-EU trade lane, warehouse or fulfilment-centre establishment, 3PL contracting, FOT licence application or renewal, acquisition of a carrier, new import/export product line, customs-warehouse or transit expansion, CEMT permit requirement or Alpine corridor operation.
Typical ScenarioA company plans professional cross-border goods transport using vehicles above 2.5 tonnes and must obtain a FOT licence; a Swiss importer must use its UID to lodge electronic customs declarations and determine whether an EU EORI is required for a direct EU customs interface; a manufacturer appoints a 3PL provider for Swiss distribution and must allocate warehouse, customs and delivery responsibilities; a forwarder plans an Alpine road-rail supply chain between Italy, Switzerland and Germany.
Professional AssistanceTypically relevant when FOT licence status is uncertain, customs classification is complex, Swiss and EU customs systems both apply, supply chain design spans several modes, providers and jurisdictions, or Alpine transit, driver, route and third-country trade rules are relevant.

Country Characteristics

Switzerland's logistics environment is shaped by its landlocked Alpine geography, central location between key EU markets, non-membership of the EU and EU Customs Union, high-value trade profile and material road/rail transit role. These conditions make customs and border design central commercial matters. FOT administers professional road-transport licensing at federal level, while FOCBS administers Swiss customs procedures and electronic declaration identity through the UID system. The distinction between Swiss UID and EU EORI is a specific operational feature for businesses that move goods across the Swiss-EU border.

Operational CultureDocumentation-led, cross-border intensive and corridor-sensitive. Carrier, UID, customs, terminal, warehouse and client data must remain consistent across Swiss, EU and counterpart-jurisdiction compliance layers.
Institutional StructureFOT issues road-transport licences valid for five years. FOCBS/BAZG administers customs and uses UID for Swiss electronic declarations. The Federal Statistical Office operates the UID system; cantonal registers and authorities are relevant to local company establishment and certain operational matters.
Classification LogicProfessional goods transport using vehicles above 2.5 tonnes is generally subject to the FOT licence regime from 1 May 2025. Domestic Swiss goods transport exclusively using vehicles over 2.5 tonnes but no more than 3.5 tonnes is exempt. International operations in that lighter range remain subject to licensing. Customs treatment is material at the Swiss-EU border and depends on tariff classification, origin, customs value and applicable procedure.
Language ExpectationGerman, French, Italian and Romansh are Switzerland's national languages. The working language depends on the canton and authority. FOT and FOCBS provide material in several national languages and selected English information. Formal submissions and commercial contracts should use terminology appropriate to the relevant canton, federal authority and trade lane.

The competent authority depends on transport mode and legal route. A single Swiss supply chain operation may interact with FOT, FOCBS, the UID register, cantonal commercial or road authorities, terminal operators, foreign EU customs administrations and sector-specific bodies because licensing, customs, vehicle, warehouse, border and route-related matters are administered through different channels.

Applicable Legislation

Swiss commercial road freight operates under the Road Transport Act and related licensing framework administered by FOT. Switzerland's professional access rules apply domestically, while international operations also interact with bilateral, EU and multilateral market-access arrangements. Swiss customs is governed by national customs law because Switzerland is outside the EU Customs Union. EU EORI is not a Swiss customs identifier but can be required when a Swiss business deals directly with EU customs systems. Freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics and 3PL arrangements are generally organised through commercial contract and general Swiss commercial law, unless a connected activity is separately regulated.

Process Flow and Decision Tree

There is no single universal logistics process because the appropriate route depends on service type, transport mode, vehicle category and whether goods cross the Swiss customs border. Nevertheless, most Swiss operations move from planning and classification into company and licence preparation, assembly of operational and financial material, customs and UID setup, formal licensing or contracting, review, decision and ongoing compliance with licence conditions, customs obligations or service agreements.

1. Define the OperationIdentify the service type (transport, forwarding, warehousing, fulfilment, contract logistics, 3PL), transport mode, vehicle category, goods type, Swiss establishment, Alpine or terminal interface, trade lane and whether movement is domestic, Swiss-EU or third-country.
2. Screen Legal TriggersAssess the activity against FOT professional transport licence thresholds, domestic lighter-vehicle exemption, Community/CEMT or bilateral permit needs, Swiss customs law, EU EORI interface and other relevant regimes.
3. Identify the Competent Authority or CounterpartyDetermine whether the matter falls to FOT, FOCBS, UID administration, a cantonal authority, an EU customs authority, a terminal, a warehouse or 3PL provider, or another party.
4. Register and Prepare EvidenceConfirm Swiss company and durable establishment; assemble good-repute, professional-capacity, financial-capacity, vehicle and corporate evidence; confirm UID and determine whether an EU EORI is required for the planned customs interface.
5. Prepare the Documentation BaseDevelop FOT licence applications, transport and warehousing contracts, Swiss and EU tariff-classification records, customs declarations, transit documents, CEMT/permit material and proposed operating conditions.
6. Submit, Contract and CompleteFile the FOT licence, customs or international permit request, establish customs access and UID use, or finalise the forwarding or warehousing agreement; address requests for clarification or supplementary material.
7. ExaminationThe authority or counterparty reviews the material, may request further evidence, and assesses establishment, good repute, financial capacity, professional capacity, customs classification, border procedure or service terms.
8. Decision and ConditionsA FOT licence, Community/CEMT permit, Swiss customs procedure, UID/EORI status or service agreement may set scope, validity, reporting duties, route conditions and other operating requirements.
9. Operate, Monitor and Manage ChangeMaintain compliance, use the correct Swiss customs identifier and declaration system, meet licence, border and reporting duties, and assess whether fleet, service scope, trade lane or goods-category changes require renewal, amendment or a new process.
Decision logic: First identify the service type, transport mode, vehicle category and whether the goods cross the Swiss customs border. Then determine the applicable FOT licence, Swiss customs, EU EORI-interface and contractual regime. Only after the operational route is identified can the competent authority or provider, documentation package and compliance timeline be reliably planned.

Timeline

Logistics setup, FOT licensing and customs preparation should be treated as part of supply chain planning rather than a late administrative step. Timing depends on the completeness of Swiss establishment, good-repute, financial and professional material, FOT processing, UID readiness, customs procedure design and route or terminal conditions. A Swiss company should ensure that its UID and Swiss electronic customs access are in place before the relevant import, export or transit movement is undertaken.

Early Planning StageTrade-lane definition, transport mode and service selection, Swiss-EU border mapping, Alpine or terminal assessment, licence, customs or contracting strategy.
Pre-Application StageSwiss company and durable-establishment preparation, good-repute, professional-capacity and financial-capacity evidence, vehicle information, UID verification, EU EORI interface analysis and customs-document collection.
Submission StageFOT road transport licence and international documentation procedures are filed with FOT; Swiss customs processes are handled through FOCBS systems using UID; EU EORI arrangements are made with the appropriate EU customs authority where relevant; forwarding or warehousing agreements are finalised.
Examination StageCompleteness review, verification of FOT professional-access conditions, Swiss UID/customs identity, EU-side EORI needs and possible requests for clarification or additional material.
Decision StageFOT licence, Community/CEMT permit, Swiss customs procedure, UID/EORI confirmation, customs authorisation, signed service agreement, refusal or other formal result.
Post-Decision StageImplementation, licence-document and vehicle control, Swiss border-declaration testing, terminal or warehouse onboarding and ongoing operational compliance management.
Change StageBefore fleet expansion, new Swiss-EU trade lanes, service-scope changes, new goods categories, altered customs procedures or a changed Swiss establishment, reassess existing licences, permits, UID/EORI positions, customs procedures and agreements.

Cross-Border Relevance

Logistics and supply chain operations in Switzerland are inherently cross-border. Switzerland is located within the European economic area but outside both the EU and the EU Customs Union. This means commercial goods flows to and from EU neighbours are border and customs-relevant even where the route is geographically short and commercially integrated. The operator must distinguish the Swiss transport licence and UID-based customs system from the EU-side Community Licence, EORI and customs procedures that may be applicable to the same shipment.

Foreign CompaniesA foreign-owned undertaking established in Switzerland is generally assessed under the same Swiss framework as a domestic operator. A FOT licence requires a real and durable Swiss seat, good repute, financial capacity and professional capacity. Foreign carriers serving Switzerland must also assess their own home-jurisdiction licence, Swiss market-access and customs position.
EU and EEA ContextSwitzerland is not an EU or EEA member but has bilateral transport arrangements with the EU. The Swiss FOT licence, EU Community Licence and other international market-access documents should be assessed separately according to the operator's establishment, vehicle, route and service model.
Swiss-EU Customs ContextSwitzerland is outside the EU Customs Union. Goods crossing the Swiss-EU border are subject to Swiss and EU customs requirements. The Swiss UID identifies the company in Swiss electronic customs declarations; EU EORI is relevant to EU-side customs interaction and may be needed for direct electronic communication with EU customs authorities.
Alpine and Transit ContextAlpine road and rail routes can involve route restrictions, border queues, customs offices, terminal interfaces, driving-time planning and intermodal alternatives. The operational route should be assessed for the specific load, direction and transport mode.
Language ConsiderationsSwiss authority interaction may be in German, French or Italian depending on the authority and canton. English information is available for selected FOT and FOCBS topics. Cross-border contracts and declarations should use terminology appropriate to each competent authority and counterparty jurisdiction.
Practical RiskAssuming that Switzerland's close commercial integration with the EU eliminates customs obligations, or that Swiss UID, EU EORI, a FOT licence and an EU Community Licence are interchangeable identifiers or authorisations without separate assessment.

Operating Constraints, Risks and Costs

The central practical risk is treating Swiss logistics licensing and customs compliance as a one-time filing exercise rather than an ongoing operational and border-compliance process. Errors in FOT licence classification, Swiss-EU customs design, UID/EORI use, route planning, financial evidence or service documentation can affect timing, cost and the ability to operate as planned.

Licence Threshold RiskThe operator may incorrectly apply the 2.5-tonne professional transport threshold, overlook the domestic-only lighter-vehicle exemption, or assume that an exemption applies to international activity where a FOT licence is required.
Swiss Establishment and Professional Access RiskA FOT licence requires a real and durable Swiss seat, good repute, financial capacity and professional capacity. Incomplete or nominal establishment and management arrangements can affect licence eligibility and continuing compliance.
UID/EORI Identity RiskSwiss UID is used for Swiss electronic customs declarations, while EORI is an EU customs identifier. Using the wrong identifier, or failing to arrange an EU EORI where direct EU customs communication is required, can disrupt the border process.
Customs and Classification RiskIncorrect tariff classification, customs value, origin treatment, procedure selection or declaration data can affect duty, VAT, release timing, security obligations and audit exposure on either side of the Swiss-EU border.
Alpine Corridor RiskTransit routes can be affected by road restrictions, heavy-vehicle charges, seasonal conditions, terminal capacity, traffic measures and available rail alternatives. These should be reflected in route, cost and delivery planning.
Cost DriversLicence and qualification costs, financial-capacity evidence, customs duties and import VAT, border administration, heavy-vehicle charges, terminal and warehouse fees, 3PL fees, IT and declaration systems, route contingencies, professional advisers and possible penalties.

FAQ

When is a Swiss road transport company licence required?Professional road transport companies carrying goods generally need a FOT licence. From 1 May 2025, the licence regime applies to professional goods transport with vehicles exceeding 2.5 tonnes. An exception applies to professional goods transport exclusively within Switzerland using vehicles over 2.5 tonnes but not exceeding 3.5 tonnes. International operations in that lighter range remain subject to licensing.
How long is a Swiss FOT licence valid?The Federal Office of Transport issues a road transport company licence valid for five years to companies with their seat in Switzerland that satisfy the professional-access conditions.
What conditions must a Swiss road transport operator meet?Under the Road Transport Act, the applicant must satisfy the criteria of good repute, financial capacity, professional capacity and a real and durable establishment in Switzerland. FOT assesses these requirements in the licence process.
Does Switzerland use EORI for its own customs declarations?No. Swiss companies use their UID to declare goods electronically in Swiss Customs systems. EORI is relevant in trade with the EU where a Swiss company directly interacts with EU customs authorities or EU electronic pre-notification systems.
Why is customs important for Switzerland-EU logistics?Switzerland is not part of the EU Customs Union. Goods crossing between Switzerland and the EU are subject to the applicable Swiss and EU customs procedures, declarations and supporting documents even where the transport route is short or the trade parties are closely integrated.
Are freight forwarding and warehousing separately licensed in Switzerland?Freight forwarding, warehousing, contract logistics and 3PL services are generally organised through commercial contract and general Swiss commercial law rather than a dedicated professional licence. Connected activities such as commercial road haulage, customs warehousing, customs representation or dangerous-goods handling may carry separate requirements.
